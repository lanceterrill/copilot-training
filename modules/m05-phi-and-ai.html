PHI & AI Rules

≈ 40 minutes · this is the module that matters most

Everything in modules 1–4 assumed you'd use Copilot well. This module is about using it lawfully in a workplace where protected health information is part of daily work. HIPAA didn't change when AI arrived — but AI changed how easy it is to break HIPAA by accident. These are the rules.

Minimum necessary, when the assistant can summarize everything

The minimum necessary standard says you use and disclose only the PHI required for the task at hand. Before Copilot, over-accessing took effort. Now one prompt can assemble every document you can open. The standard didn't get weaker — your reach got longer. So the discipline moves into the prompt:

  • Scope prompts to the task: name the specific file or thread rather than asking Copilot to sweep everything.
  • Don't use Copilot to browse patient information out of curiosity. Curiosity access is a HIPAA violation with or without AI.
  • When a summary comes back with more patient detail than the task needs, trim it before it goes anywhere.
PHI rule · the approved-tool line

PHI goes only into tools this organization has approved for PHI. Our signed-in Microsoft 365 Copilot, inside our tenant, is on that list. Personal AI accounts, free consumer chatbots, browser AI extensions, and "just this once" tools are not. The test is simple: if you'd have to explain why you used it, don't use it.

PHI rule · outputs are disclosures

A Copilot-drafted email, summary, or document containing PHI is a use or disclosure of PHI the moment you share it. Check who's on the recipient line, what details the draft pulled in, and whether every one of those details is necessary. "Copilot wrote it" is not a defense — you sent it.

PHI rule · oversharing cuts both ways

If Copilot ever surfaces patient information you don't believe your role should access, treat it as a reportable permissions problem: stop, don't copy or share what you saw, and report it to the Privacy Officer the same day. You will never be penalized for reporting access that was too broad.

What never goes in a prompt

  • PHI in any web-grounded chat (the web toggle in Copilot Chat) — work grounding only.
  • PHI in any tool outside the approved list, including personal devices signed into personal accounts.
  • Passwords, access codes, or security details of any system — AI tools are not vaults.
  • Information about coworkers' health, discipline, or personal life that your role doesn't require.

AI-specific failure modes to watch for

  • Confident errors. Copilot can state wrong information fluently. Clinical, billing, and policy facts get verified against the source before use — always.
  • Context bleed. Summaries can merge details from multiple patients or matters into one paragraph. Read summaries critically before forwarding.
  • Transcription creep. Recorded meetings become searchable text. Only record or transcribe meetings where that's permitted and announced.
  • Automation lull. The tenth AI draft gets less scrutiny than the first. It should get the same.

When something goes wrong

Wrong recipient, unexpected access, PHI pasted somewhere it shouldn't be — the steps are the same:

  • Stop. Don't send more, don't delete evidence, don't try to quietly fix it.
  • Report the same day to your supervisor and the Privacy Officer. Fast reporting shrinks harm and is required by our breach procedures.
  • Document what happened while it's fresh: what, when, who may have seen it.
